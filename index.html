Research

  1. Firms and Special Economic Zones in the Philippines Working paper.
    Abstract

    This thesis provides a firm-level evaluation of special economic zones in the Philippines. We assemble a dataset that merges annual manufacturing establishment surveys with the universe of firm- and product-level export transactions and information on economic zone participation for 18,890 firms in the Philippines, 650 of which operate inside special economic zones. We examine the effects of zone location on firm size, productivity, and export diversification using a range of quasi-experimental approaches: difference-in-differences, endogenous switching regressions, and coarsened exact matching. We find the following: (i) while zone location is associated with increases in sales, employment, and export diversification, firms that locate in economic zones do not become more productive, nor do they diversify into more sophisticated products; (ii) the results are driven by firms in private zones, and publicly governed zones do not exhibit any size and productivity enhancing effects; (iii) zone location is associated with increases in export orientation, imports, and foreign ownership, but not in investments, wages, or research, and (iv) these zones do not benefit unregistered firms in surrounding localities.

  2. Who Exports, Diversifies, and Upgrades in the Philippines? (with Jesus Felipe and Nanditha Mathew). Under review, The Journal of Development Studies.
    Abstract

    We study the margins of exporting among manufacturing firms in the Philippines – a developing country that has largely missed the episode of export-oriented industrialization in Asia. We use a rich dataset that merges manufacturing plant surveys with the universe of firm- and product-level export transactions to construct firm-level measures of export diversification and product upgrading. We use endogenous switching models to estimate the impact of these margins on firm-level growth across various export regimes. We find the following: (i) exporting positively affects employment and sales growth, but it is negatively associated with labor productivity growth. For non-exporters, the effects of exporting are insignificant across all measures of firm growth; (ii) the introduction of new products into a firm’s export portfolio significantly enhances firm growth in terms of employment, sales, and labor productivity growth. However, if non-diversifying firms had diversified, they would have experienced productivity declines; (iii) introducing new products that are more complex than those existing in a firm’s export portfolio positively impacts labor productivity growth. The productivity benefits of upgrading appear to extend beyond firms that actually upgrade, suggesting that the returns to upgrading are uniform across firms. These findings highlight the importance of not just participating in trade, but of upgrading and diversification as key mechanisms through which trade contributes to growth. We discuss the implications of these findings for trade and industrial policy in developing Asia.
